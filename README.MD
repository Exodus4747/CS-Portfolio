This is the final deliverable from CS 250. Over the course I worked through a single project, a travel booking application for a client called SNHU Travel, taking a different role on the Scrum team each module: Scrum Master, Product Owner, tester, and developer. The retrospective pulls that work together and evaluates how the Scrum-Agile approach held up.

---

## How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating user stories help with this?

I start by listening to what people actually say and then separating what they want from how they think it should work. In this course I ran a focus group as the Product Owner, and people did not ask for features in a form I could build. They described frustrations. Turning those into user stories, written as "as a type of user, I want to do something so that I can achieve some goal," forced me to name who wants it and why, which is the part that usually gets lost when you jump straight to a feature list.

The piece that made stories actually buildable was acceptance criteria written as numbers. "Show ten destinations." "Update within two seconds." "Pages load in three seconds." Before that, "done" was an opinion, and two people could look at the same screen and disagree about whether it was finished. After that, it was a fact anyone could check. Sizing the stories as small, medium, or large and putting them in priority order also mattered, because it meant the largest one, a personalized destination list built from a traveler's profile and booking history, could sit below smaller stories that shipped right away instead of blocking everything behind it.

## How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I like to understand the whole problem before writing much, but this course pushed me toward getting something running early and improving it, rather than planning it all up front. Building in increments meant I had working software at the end of every module, and problems showed up while they were still cheap to fix.

Three practices I want to keep:

- **Acceptance criteria with real numbers, written before the work starts.** This is the single highest-value habit I picked up. It prevents the argument about whether something is finished.
- **Retrospectives.** A fixed time to ask what helped, what did not, and what to change next. Without it, teams repeat the same problems and nobody ever says so.
- **Limiting work in progress.** Finishing things before starting new ones. A pile of half-done work looks like progress and is not.

The flexibility is the real payoff. Midway through the project the client shifted toward wellness and detox travel, which invalidated the destinations I had already chosen. Because the work was in small pieces with a sprint boundary coming up, that change cost one sprint of rework. Under a waterfall approach it would have been a formal change request against signed-off requirements, and it probably would have been pushed to a later release.

## What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Mostly it means making your work visible and asking the questions you are tempted to answer yourself.

The clearest lesson I took from this course came from a mistake. One story said ten destinations and the wireframe showed five, and that difference was never settled in writing. I noticed it as the tester and did nothing about it. When I reached the developer role, I picked five on my own so I could keep moving. Content decisions were the Product Owner's to make, not mine. The right move was to raise it the moment I saw it, and a one-line comment on the story would have closed it. A decision that lives in one person's head is a decision the next person has to make again, usually differently.

So a good team member writes things down where the team can see them, asks specific closed questions of named people instead of broad ones to everyone, and respects the boundaries of the roles. Those boundaries are not bureaucracy. The Product Owner decides what matters, the developer decides how to build it, and the tester decides whether it is actually done. When I kept those separate the work got better, and when I blurred them it got worse.
